A wider frame

Six ideas to carry
into your week

There is no single correct relationship to work. These ideas invite you to notice what is happening and choose what fits your circumstances.

01 / IDENTITY

You are a person, not a job title

When work becomes the main source of identity, a setback can feel like a verdict on the whole self. Interests and roles outside work give identity more room and resilience.

02 / MEANING

A calling is not a requirement

Work may be meaningful without being a passion, and meaningful work can still be tiring or imperfect. Expecting one job to meet every need can turn ordinary limits into personal failure.

03 / WORTH

Your output is not your value

Productivity can describe what got done; it cannot measure a person’s dignity. Rest, care, friendship, play, and being here matter even when they produce nothing.

04 / CONNECTION

Belonging needs more than an office

Work friendships can be real and valuable. A broader community can offer connection that does not depend on a role or employer.

05 / SYSTEMS

Overwork is not only an individual problem

Personal boundaries help, but workload, staffing, pay, power, and workplace norms shape what is possible. Sustainable change may need both personal choices and organizational support.

06 / “ENOUGH”

Define success in your own terms

More status, income, or responsibility is not automatically better. Naming what “good enough” means this season can help you see whether a goal serves you—or keeps moving.

Unpacking familiar stories

Ideas worth holding
more lightly

Open a card to consider a common belief about work and a more flexible way to look at it.

“If I love my work, it won’t feel like work.”

Interest and effort can coexist. Even meaningful work includes routine, conflict, fatigue, and trade-offs. Liking parts of a job does not obligate you to give it every part of yourself.

“My workplace is my family.”

Colleagues can care deeply about one another, while an organization still has roles, goals, and economic interests. Warm relationships and clear expectations can coexist.

“A prestigious job means I’m succeeding.”

Status is one signal, not a complete account of a good life. Consider what the role costs in time, health, relationships, freedom, or alignment with your values.

“If I set better boundaries, the problem is solved.”

Boundaries can protect some time and attention, yet workers do not control every demand. Notice which changes are yours to make and which need support or a wider workplace response.

A practical reframe

Try asking a different question

Instead of “What do I do?” ask what you enjoy, care about, or want to make room for. No single answer has to define you.

For your own reflection

Outside work, what makes time feel worthwhile? Which parts of your week reflect your values—and which are running on autopilot?

For a work conversation

What does “good enough” performance look like here? What takes priority when everything cannot be done? What would make this workload sustainable?
